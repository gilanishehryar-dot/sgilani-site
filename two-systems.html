Two Systems: What UK Housing Boards and Gulf Investment Committees Get Wrong About Each Other

UK housing boards over-govern and under-decide. Gulf investment committees decide fast and govern late. Each system’s strength is the other’s blind spot.

Shehryar Gilani, CFA FCMA MRICS·November 2026·7 min read

I spent 18 years inside one of the UK’s largest housing groups, and the last few years inside Gulf giga-projects and a Public Investment Fund-backed master developer. Both systems approve billions in long-dated capital. Both are full of capable people. And they fail in almost exactly opposite directions.

UK housing boards tend to over-govern and under-decide. Gulf investment committees tend to decide fast and govern late. Each system’s strength is the other’s blind spot, and each would make better decisions if it borrowed what the other does well.

The UK failure mode: assurance without decision

A UK housing association board works inside a dense web of accountability: a regulator that grades governance and financial viability, lenders with covenants, and a long memory of what happens when a development programme overreaches. At L&Q, where I led financial planning against approximately £5.5bn of debt, interest cover was a critical measure of financial resilience. The board had to balance development ambitions against the capacity to service debt, maintain covenant headroom and protect investment in existing homes.

That discipline is real and valuable. Assumptions are tested. Downside cases are standard. Boards know their covenant headroom to the decimal point.

But it carries a cost. The Board pack grows with every cycle, because adding analysis feels safer than taking a decision. Papers are written to show that risks have been considered rather than to ask for a choice. Approvals come back for a second and third look. By the time a scheme is approved, the market that justified it may have moved.

That lost time is rarely priced. When I modelled a six-month delay on an illustrative 200-unit scheme with a 17.1% internal rate of return, the answer depended on when it landed. Before mobilisation, in a rising market, the return fell to 14.6%. After the contractor was appointed, in a flat market, it fell to 9.8%, and stretched to twelve months, to 5%. Nobody ever decided to accept that. It accumulated one review at a time.

The risk in this system isn’t recklessness. It’s delay, and the quiet value lost when good schemes arrive late or never.

The Gulf failure mode: momentum without ownership

A Gulf giga-project starts from the opposite end. The ambition is set first, often at national scale, and the capital is there to match it. Decisions move quickly. Programmes are designed as portfolios from day one, with infrastructure, placemaking and phasing planned together. In the Gulf, I’ve seen how quickly strategic priorities can translate into material investment decisions, with multi-billion development programmes requiring decisions on phasing, capital allocation and commercial viability, often across several interdependent projects.

That pace is a genuine advantage. UK boards would recognise few of the delays they live with.

The weakness is that governance often catches up after the decision rather than shaping it. Assumptions travel from consultant reports into financial models without a named owner. Timing is optimistic because the ambition is fixed. When a key input moves, nobody is sure whether it is material, or who should decide. Case by case, the numbers drift, and the portfolio’s capital need grows faster than anyone approved.

The risk here isn’t caution. It’s momentum: decisions that are hard to stop because nobody defined the point at which they should be.

The counterweight is a process that can say “not yet”, quickly. In my current role I led the assessment of a five-star hotel of about £90–100m (SAR 450–500m), challenged its assumptions and recommended deferring it until Phase 1 was complete, so it would gain from the place Phase 1 creates rather than pay to arrive first. The Management Committee agreed. The hotel wasn’t wrong; its timing was.

What each system should borrow

UK housing boards should borrowGulf investment committees should borrow
A portfolio view of capital, not scheme-by-scheme approvalNamed ownership of every critical assumption
Decision papers that ask for a choice, with a recommendationDownside and break-even cases as standard, not on request
Speed on decisions within agreed limitsTolerances that define when a moved assumption must return for a decision
Ambition in placemaking and phasingStop rules agreed at approval, not after the overrun

The shared fix: govern the inputs, then move fast

The two systems look different, but the cure is the same. Speed and governance aren’t in tension. Governance is what makes speed safe.

In practice that means four disciplines, which I set out in the Decision Integrity System:

  1. Governed assumptions. Every critical input has an owner, a source and a tolerance. The assumptions register is the simplest way to start.
  2. A clear decision boundary. Risk appetite and tolerance define what management can decide and what must go back to the Board.
  3. A model built to be interrogated. Timing, price, cost and funding can be tested in the meeting, not two days later.
  4. A monthly rhythm. A business review that shows what moved, why, and what decision it now needs.

A UK board that adopts these can delegate more and decide faster, because it trusts the boundaries. A Gulf committee that adopts them keeps its pace, because it knows the moment a decision needs to be revisited.

Questions for your next Board meeting

  1. Which of our last five major approvals came back for a second review, and what did the extra time change?
  2. For our largest case, who owns the five assumptions that move the return most?
  3. Do we know, before approval, the point at which we would stop or re-scope?
  4. Is our capital envelope set before cases are approved, or discovered after?
